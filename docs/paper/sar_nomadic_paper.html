Coverage and Traffic Behavior of a UAV-Mounted 5G Base Station for Multi-Robot Search and Rescue: An Emulation Study

Innocent Nhlanhla Makhubela and Joyce Mwangama
Department of Electrical Engineering, University of Cape Town, South Africa
makhubelainnocentnhlanhla@gmail.com

Abstract—UAV-mounted base stations ("nomadic nodes") promise on-demand 5G connectivity for ground robots in search-and-rescue (SAR) operations, but system-level evidence of how such links behave under realistic mobility and traffic remains scarce. We present an emulation testbed that closes the loop between robot mobility and a real 5G protocol stack: robot and UAV motion in a physics simulator continuously drives an air-to-ground (A2G) path-loss model whose output is injected at runtime into an OpenAirInterface 5G standalone system serving two ground UEs, over which genuine IP traffic — bulk TCP transfer, a fixed-rate UDP video proxy, and small-packet command-and-control (C2) probes — is measured in both directions. Across four measurement campaigns (≈2,100 samples spanning 6–140 m of range and 20–60 m of UAV altitude) the emulated channel tracks the analytic A2G model within 0.6 dB, and the measurements yield five operationally significant findings: (i) UAV altitude, not horizontal position, is the primary coverage control, buying 10 dB of link margin at mid-range and nearly doubling the safe operating radius; (ii) heterogeneous UE link budgets produce qualitatively different throughput envelopes; (iii) the three traffic classes fail in three distinct ways, with C2 round-trip latency spiking before packet loss — an intrinsic early-warning signal; (iv) the coverage edge is a hard cliff with hysteresis: radio link failure is not repaired by returning to previously covered positions; and (v) repositioning the aerial node can strand slow ground users outside its moving coverage zone. We distill these into operating guidelines for nomadic-node deployments and release the testbed methodology, including a network-namespace technique that ensures downlink traffic genuinely traverses the emulated radio.

Index Terms—UAV communications, nomadic base station, 5G, search and rescue, air-to-ground channel, OpenAirInterface, network emulation.

I. Introduction

Search-and-rescue operations routinely unfold where terrestrial network infrastructure is damaged or absent. A UAV carrying a base station — a nomadic node — can restore connectivity on demand, serving ground robots that stream sensor data and video to a command post while receiving waypoints and safety-critical commands in return. The concept is well established in the literature on aerial communication platforms [1], [2], [3], and standardized channel models exist for low-altitude platforms [4]. What is less available is system-level evidence: how does a real 5G protocol stack — with its scheduler, HARQ, adaptive modulation and radio-link-failure machinery — actually behave when the geometry between an aerial cell and mobile ground users changes continuously, and what does that imply for how such a node should be operated?

This paper contributes such evidence from an emulation testbed built around three requirements. First, coupling: robot and UAV mobility must drive the radio channel continuously, not as precomputed traces. Second, protocol realism: the RAN must be a real 5G SA implementation, not an abstraction, so that phenomena like MCS adaptation, HARQ retransmission and RLF emerge from the stack itself. Third, traffic honesty: measured traffic must genuinely traverse the emulated radio in both directions — a property that, as we show, is easy to violate silently when an entire testbed shares one host.

Our contributions are: (1) the testbed architecture, including a runtime channel-injection bridge and a network-namespace isolation technique with which we caught and eliminated a class of invalid downlink measurements; (2) an empirical characterization of coverage, throughput, latency and command-channel reliability across ≈2,100 samples of 3-D geometry, validated against the analytic A2G model to within 0.6 dB; and (3) five findings with direct operational consequences for nomadic-node deployments, including a climb-first repositioning rule and an intrinsic early-warning signal for coverage-edge approach.

II. Related Work

Al-Hourani et al. derived the widely used LoS-probability model for low-altitude platforms and showed that an optimal altitude balances free-space loss against shadowing [1]; our channel model adopts their sigmoid formulation. Surveys by Zeng et al. [2] and Mozaffari et al. [3] catalogue design opportunities for UAV-assisted networks, and 3GPP has studied aerial channel characteristics for cellular support of UAVs [4]. These works are predominantly analytic or link-level; our study complements them with protocol-stack-in-the-loop measurements. On the tooling side, OpenAirInterface provides an open 3GPP-compliant RAN implementation [5] whose rfsimulator backend and runtime channel-model interface make controlled experiments reproducible; Open5GS supplies the 5G core [6], and Gazebo the robotics simulation [7]. To our knowledge the combination — physics-simulated multi-robot mobility continuously modulating a live 5G SA stack carrying measured bidirectional application traffic — has not previously been characterized in the SAR context.

III. Testbed Architecture

A. Mobility-coupled channel emulation

Two TurtleBot3 ground robots (UEs alpha and bravo) and one x500 UAV (the gNB) move in a 500 m open-field Gazebo world. A bridge process subscribes to the world pose stream and, every 0.5 s, computes per-link A2G path loss as free-space loss at 3.32 GHz plus an elevation-angle-dependent LoS/NLoS excess following [1] (A = 9.61, B = 0.16, ηLoS = 1 dB, ηNLoS = 20 dB). Deltas relative to a 50 m reference are added to per-link baselines (alpha 20/15 dB UL/DL, bravo 25/20 dB — the 5 dB offset emulates a weaker device class), smoothed with an exponential moving average (α = 0.3), and injected into the running gNB and UE processes through OAI's channel-model telnet interface, together with a propagation-delay offset. Sub-reference distances legitimately produce sub-baseline path loss (floored at 10 dB); the high side is capped below OAI's radio-link-failure region.

B. Traffic honesty: the loopback pitfall and its fix

Because gNB, UEs, core and measurement tools share one host, the UE tunnel addresses are initially local to that host — and Linux delivers locally addressed traffic via loopback. Every base-to-robot packet (commands, TCP acknowledgments, any reverse-mode test) silently bypassed the radio: a "downlink" iperf3 reported 1.4–5.2 Gbit/s while the gNB's own MAC counter showed 0.15 Mbit/s actually scheduled. Our own initial video measurement fell into exactly this trap — configured in reverse (downlink) mode, it logged its full ≈100 Mbit/s offered rate at every distance, carrying no channel information whatsoever; it was reoriented as an uplink stream (matching the SAR scenario, where video flows robot-to-base) before any data reported here was collected. We eliminate the artifact by moving each UE's TUN interface into a per-robot network namespace after attach (the modem process keeps its file descriptor across the move, so the RAN is untouched). Afterwards, downlink traffic measurably traverses UPF → gNB → radio — 30 Mbit/s at the application against 52.9 Mbit/s mid-burst on the gNB DL counter at MCS 27 — and a plain ping to a robot constitutes a true bidirectional over-air round trip. We flag this pitfall explicitly because single-host emulation is common and the failure is silent.

C. Measurement suite

Robots drive continuously (differential-drive control at 1 m/s, randomized headings) while a sampler cycles per robot: TCP goodput (2 s test with 1 s slow-start warm-up excluded), a fixed 8 Mbit/s UDP uplink stream as a video-feed proxy (delivered rate, jitter, loss), uplink-path ping, and a C2 probe — five 64-byte pings from base to robot emulating waypoint or emergency-stop delivery. Each sample row records the true mid-window 3-D geometry of both robots and the UAV. Radio-side ground truth (per-UE goodput, HARQ round distribution, MCS, SNR) is available from the gNB's live statistics and was used to validate every traffic path. A per-altitude safe-radius controller keeps robots inside the model-predicted coverage edge (Section V-B), with an emergency recall for robots stranded by UAV repositioning.

A. Data hygiene

Three artifact classes were identified and handled. (1) During outages the modem-log parser holds its last values, so RSRP/SINR are invalidated whenever ping loss is total. (2) In Survey A, an iperf3 server left mid-test by a timed-out client rejects the next connection; 19% of samples logged zero throughput on a demonstrably live link. These are excluded (zero TCP with zero ping loss), and the root cause was fixed — retry with settle intervals — after which Survey B and the Nomadic campaign recorded zero artifacts. (3) The injected-path-loss log trails commanded positions by one smoothing step; model comparisons use per-row geometry.

V. Results

A. Emulation fidelity

Fig. 2 shows measured injected path loss against distance for Survey B at fixed altitude; Fig. 3 generalizes to the Nomadic campaign as a parity plot, since no single distance curve exists when altitude varies. Agreement with the analytic model is 0.4–0.6 dB mean absolute error across all campaigns and the full 3-D geometry range — the behaviors reported below arise from the physics under study rather than emulation artifacts. The 5 dB device-class offset between UEs is cleanly resolved throughout.

B. Altitude is the primary coverage control

Table II isolates the altitude effect: identical robots at identical horizontal distances while the UAV hovered at different heights. Close to the node, altitude is nearly irrelevant. At mid-range, climbing from 20 m to 60 m buys 10 dB of margin and ≈50% more TCP goodput; the mechanism is elevation angle converting the link from NLoS-dominated to line-of-sight [1]. Expressed as a coverage rule — holding the weaker UE ≈5 dB below the RLF region — the safe operating radius is 86 m at 20 m altitude, 117 m at 40 m, and 149 m at 60 m.

Finding 1: a nomadic node should not chase its users horizontally — climbing is the faster, cheaper coverage control, and costs almost nothing when users are close.

C. Device class defines the envelope

The two UEs experience qualitatively different networks. Alpha (stronger budget) is distance-limited: goodput declines smoothly from ≈11 to ≈6 Mbit/s across the range (Fig. 4). Bravo (5 dB weaker) is budget-limited: pinned at ≈7.8 Mbit/s at every distance — its own radio, not geometry, sets the ceiling — and it reaches the coverage cliff ≈5 m earlier.

Finding 2: coverage planning for heterogeneous fleets cannot use one number — each device class has its own envelope, and the weakest radio defines the team's operating radius.

D. Three traffic classes, three failure modes

Inside the envelope, the fixed-rate video proxy is delivered at full rate in 100% of samples of Survey B and the Nomadic campaign — and vanishes entirely at the edge: streaming has no degraded middle state. TCP bends gracefully (11 → 5 → 1 Mbit/s) before the cliff. C2 delivery is the most robust of all — median round trip 38 ms, 95th percentile ≤ 58 ms, indifferent to distance and altitude — until immediately before the edge, where the round trip inflates by an order of magnitude (250 ms to 1.2 s) before any packet is lost. An operator therefore experiences edge approach as a fixed sequence: video drops out, transfers crawl, command latency spikes.

Finding 3: the C2 latency spike is an intrinsic early-warning signal — the network announces the coverage edge before it is crossed.

E. The edge is a cliff with hysteresis

At ≈38–43 dB injected path loss (130–135 m at 40 m altitude) the link does not degrade further — it fails, with RNTI churn at the gNB. Critically, the failure is sticky: in the edge-crossing campaign a robot that retreated to positions where its link had been flawless found it still dead (Fig. 5), and one UE never re-attached without a RAN restart. Radio-link failure is a protocol state, not a channel state; improving the geometry afterwards does not undo it.

Finding 4: at the coverage edge a robot cannot reliably be stopped, recalled or re-tasked — the edge must be avoided predictively, not reactively. The UAV climbing extends the edge outward instantly, faster than any ground robot can drive back in.

F. A moving node endangers its own users

When the UAV relocated ≈50 m horizontally, robots at the far periphery were instantaneously outside the relocated coverage zone; in an unguarded trial this destroyed both radio links within minutes via the mechanism of Section V-E. During the guarded 3-hour campaign the protective controller intervened seven times — each a link loss that would otherwise have occurred. The data implies the safe maneuver directly: climb first (expanding the radius so every user is inside), translate, then descend.

Finding 5: nomadic repositioning is not free — coverage moves with the node and slow ground assets cannot follow. Climb–move–descend coordinates the node's motion with its users.

VI. Discussion

Taken together, the findings invert a common mental model of aerial base stations as "coverage that follows you." Coverage follows the node — and the node's altitude, not its ground position, is the lever an operator (or an autonomous controller) should reach for first. Because the failure mode at the boundary is unrecoverable in place (Finding 4), any control policy must be predictive; conveniently, the system itself supplies the necessary signals without added instrumentation: C2 round-trip inflation (Finding 3) and, at the radio layer, HARQ retransmission-rate growth precede failure consistently in our data. These properties — a controllable lever, a hard constraint, and observable leading indicators — are precisely the structure needed for the learning-based nomadic-node control that motivates this testbed; the ≈2,100-sample corpus with labeled outage events collected here is intended as its training substrate, and is the subject of ongoing work by collaborators.

VII. Limitations

The RAN operates over OAI's rfsimulator: path loss and propagation delay are injected at runtime, but fast fading beyond the configured channel profiles is not present, and results at the numeric edge (exact cliff path-loss values, absolute throughput ceilings of ≈20–24 Mbit/s UL) are specific to this configuration (n78, 106 PRB, single cell, two UEs). The UAV holds altitude as a static body rather than under flight dynamics; robot speeds (1 m/s) exceed the real TurtleBot3 specification. TCP acknowledgment paths traverse the radio after the namespace fix, but the uplink-path ping metric remains asymmetric by construction and is labeled as such. Physical-layer indicators require care in this environment. The downlink channel model itself is verifiably active — a controlled injection of 55 dB downlink path loss extinguished C2 delivery within seconds, recovering on restoration — but per-sample UE-reported SS-RSRP/SS-SINR values are unusable as channel observables: the modem emits measurement reports only sparsely, so scraped values are dominated by stale carry-over (near-constant across 25 dB of injected variation), and on the uplink side closed-loop power control deliberately flattens the gNB-observed SNR. Signal strength is therefore reported as emulation ground truth (transmit reference minus injected path loss), and link-quality conclusions rest on end-to-end traffic measurements cross-checked against gNB MAC statistics. Sticky RLF durations depend on OAI's re-establishment implementation and may differ across stack versions. None of these caveats, we argue, affects the direction of the five findings, all of which follow from geometry, link budget and protocol-state mechanisms that carry over to physical deployments.

VIII. Conclusion

We presented a mobility-coupled 5G emulation testbed for UAV-mounted base stations serving ground robots, validated its channel emulation to within 0.6 dB of the analytic A2G model across three hours of continuously varying 3-D geometry, and reported five findings with direct operational force: altitude is the primary coverage control; device class defines per-robot envelopes; traffic classes fail in distinct, ordered ways that provide early warning; the coverage edge is a hard boundary with hysteresis; and node repositioning endangers peripheral users unless coordinated. Future work extends the testbed to PX4-in-the-loop flight dynamics, multi-cell handover, and — using the corpus collected here — learned predictive control of the nomadic node itself.
