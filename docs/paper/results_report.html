Results Report · Phase 1

Geometry-Aware UAV-Assisted 5G for Multi-Robot Search & Rescue

What a repositioning aerial base station does to the radio links of ground robots — measured on a protocol-accurate 5G testbed, in open-field line-of-sight conditions.

Author  I. N. Makhubela (UCT) Campaign  2026-07-12, 7.5 h Samples  2,222 (2 robots × 1,111) Environment  open field, LoS Status  draft for review

A drone carrying a 5G base station flies over two ground robots that are streaming video and telemetry back to a command post. As the drone moves — changing both its map position and its altitude — and as the robots roam, the radio link between them constantly changes. This report measures exactly how, using real 5G software (not a formula) carrying real network traffic, and turns the numbers into five plain-language lessons for how such a "nomadic node" should be flown.

01 What was measured, in plain terms

The testbed couples three things that normally live apart: a physics simulator (Gazebo) moving the drone and robots; a real 5G stack (OpenAirInterface + Open5GS) with a genuine scheduler, modulation control, and link-failure machinery; and real IP traffic flowing over the emulated radio. Robot and drone motion continuously reshapes the air-to-ground channel, which changes what the traffic actually experiences. Nothing here is a closed-form estimate — every throughput and latency number is a real transfer over a real protocol stack.

Two robots act as 5G handsets (UEs). We call them Alpha and Bravo. They are identical except that Bravo's radio is deliberately weakened by 5 dB — it stands in for a cheaper or more power-constrained device, so we can see how a mixed-quality fleet behaves. The drone is the base station (gNB). Three traffic types are measured every ~20 s per robot: a bulk upload (TCP, "sensor data dump"), a fixed 8 Mbit/s video feed (UDP, "live camera"), and small command pings (base→robot, "waypoints and emergency-stop").

The campaign has two parts: a Fixed run (drone parked at 40 m, robots roam — isolates the effect of distance) and a Nomadic run (drone flies a 9-waypoint tour across altitudes 20/40/60 m and four ground positions — isolates the effect of the drone's own movement).

05 Reading the results — three things the numbers say

A. Why is Alpha still fast when it's "far" from the drone?

This looks wrong at first — Alpha hits 13–14 Mbit/s at over 100 m from the drone. The resolution is that horizontal distance on the map is not the distance that matters to the radio. What matters is the 3-D geometry: the slant line to the drone and, above all, the elevation angle. A steep look-angle means a clean line of sight and almost no excess loss; a shallow one skims along the ground and piles on loss.

The drone at 60 m is slightly farther in raw slant distance (125 vs 118 m) yet the link is far better — because the steeper 29° angle buys a cleaner line of sight. Add Alpha's 5 dB device advantage and its 2-second TCP test saturating the rfsim ceiling whenever loss stays under ~16 dB, and you get "fast even when far." The takeaway: a robot far across the map but under a high drone is not, to the radio, far at all. This is the mechanism behind the whole coverage story.

B. Altitude is the control knob — climbing beats chasing

The clearest result of the campaign. Holding the robots at a fixed distance and only moving the drone up:

  • Close robots (20–50 m): altitude barely matters — throughput ~11–12 Mbit/s at any height.
  • Mid-range (60–90 m): climbing 20→60 m cuts path loss by 12 dB and nearly triples throughput (4.4 → 11.8 Mbit/s).
  • Far (90–120 m): the same climb raises throughput almost fivefold (2.4 → 11.6 Mbit/s).

Expressed as an operating rule, the safe radius — the distance within which even the weak robot stays comfortably connected — grows from 101 m at 20 m altitude to 163 m at 60 m. So when robots start to struggle, the drone's first move should be to climb, not chase them across the ground — climbing is faster than flying laterally and extends coverage to everyone at once.

C. The coverage edge is a cliff, and Figure 2 shows where it bites

Throughput does not fade gently to zero as robots move out — it holds near the ceiling, then collapses over a few metres at the edge of coverage. The video feed makes this starkest: 100% delivered inside the envelope, then nothing. The purpose of Figure 2 is to show where in a real mission the failures actually happen. It plots the whole 6-hour run over time; the × marks are outages. They do not scatter randomly through the mission — they cluster tightly at the geometric coverage edge, and almost all of them belong to the weak robot.

Two operational consequences follow. First, because the command channel's latency spikes before it drops packets (row 7 of the raw table shows a 144 ms command RTT while throughput was still fine), the network gives an early warning before the cliff — a signal a controller can act on. Second, recovering from a full radio-link failure is not instant; the safe-radius controller therefore treats the edge as a line never to be crossed reactively, which is why the timeline shows so few outages despite six hours of aggressive roaming.

07 The main caveat, and where this goes next

Every result here comes from an open, obstacle-free field with an unbroken line of sight between drone and robots. That is a deliberate choice: it is the cleanest setting in which to isolate the altitude / distance / device-budget relationships, and it is the right place to establish a trustworthy baseline. But it is also the easy case. The quantitative edges reported here — the safe radii, the cliff distances — should be read as best-case, line-of-sight figures.

Next phase

Work now moves to non-line-of-sight and cluttered terrains: urban blocks, forest canopy, rubble, and building shadowing. There, multipath and blockage reshape the coverage edge, and the altitude lever becomes double-edged — a higher drone sees over some obstacles but lengthens the slant path to others. Re-measuring the five findings under those conditions, and characterising how much of the clean-field coverage rule survives, is the central question of the next phase.

Also queued: a dedicated coverage-edge stress test to quantify recovery time after a link failure under the corrected physics; a CU/DU split to bring the F1/midhaul metrics online; and eventual validation against a software-defined-radio hardware bench to measure the simulation-to-hardware gap directly.
