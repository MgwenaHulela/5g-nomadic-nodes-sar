Altitude as the Primary Coverage Control for UAV-Mounted 5G Serving Ground Robots: A Protocol-in-the-Loop Emulation Study in Open-Field Line-of-Sight Conditions

Innocent N. Makhubela and Joyce Mwangama
Department of Electrical Engineering, University of Cape Town, Cape Town, South Africa

Abstract—UAV-mounted base stations ("nomadic nodes") can restore on-demand 5G connectivity to ground robots in search-and-rescue (SAR) operations, but system-level evidence of how such air-to-ground (A2G) links behave under continuous three-dimensional mobility and realistic traffic is scarce, and most prior work is analytic or link-level. We present an emulation testbed that couples a physics simulator to a real 5G standalone protocol stack: robot and UAV motion continuously drive an A2G path-loss model whose output is injected at runtime into an OpenAirInterface gNB and two UEs, over which genuine TCP, fixed-rate UDP video, and command-and-control (C2) traffic are measured in both directions. In a 7.5-hour open-field, line-of-sight (LoS) campaign of 2,222 samples spanning 4–140 m of range and 20–60 m of UAV altitude, the emulated channel tracks the analytic model to within 1.2 dB mean absolute error, is independently corroborated by UE-reported RSRP (correlation −0.91 to −0.99), and is cross-validated by the near-identical throughput–distance curves of two independent runs at equal altitude. The results yield a clear operational conclusion: UAV altitude, not horizontal repositioning, is the primary coverage control — climbing from 20 m to 60 m at mid-range recovers 12 dB of link margin and triples throughput, and moves the coverage cliff outward from ~70 m to ~130 m, extending the safe operating radius from 101 m to 163 m. We further report that a 5 dB device-budget gap relocates the coverage boundary and multiplies the outage rate (27 outages on the weaker UE versus one on the stronger, over identical trajectories); that traffic classes fail in an ordered sequence providing early warning; and that repositioning the node endangers peripheral users unless coordinated. We document in detail three silent-failure pitfalls of single-host RAN emulation and their remedies. All findings are explicitly scoped to open-field LoS; extension to non-line-of-sight (NLoS) and cluttered terrain is the subject of ongoing work.

Index Terms—UAV communications, nomadic base station, 5G NR, air-to-ground channel, search and rescue, OpenAirInterface, network emulation, coverage.

I. Introduction

Search-and-rescue operations frequently take place where terrestrial network infrastructure is damaged, congested, or absent. A UAV carrying a base station — a nomadic node — can restore connectivity on demand, serving ground robots that stream sensor data and live video to a command post while receiving waypoints and safety-critical commands in return [1]–[3]. The concept is well established, and standardized A2G channel models for low-altitude platforms exist [4], [5]. What remains comparatively scarce is system-level evidence: how does a complete 5G protocol stack — with its scheduler, link adaptation, HARQ, and radio-link-failure (RLF) machinery — behave when the three-dimensional geometry between an aerial cell and its mobile ground users changes continuously, and what does that imply for how the node should be flown?

This paper answers that question empirically. We build a testbed on three principles. First, coupling: robot and UAV mobility must drive the radio channel continuously and in real time, not as pre-computed traces. Second, protocol realism: the RAN must be a real 5G standalone implementation, so that link adaptation, HARQ, and RLF emerge from the stack rather than from an abstraction. Third, traffic honesty: measured traffic must genuinely traverse the emulated radio in both directions — a property, we show, that is surprisingly easy to violate silently on a single-host testbed.

Our contributions are: (1) the coupled testbed architecture, including the geometry-to-path-loss pipeline and its runtime injection into the live stack; (2) a detailed account of three silent-failure pitfalls encountered during construction — a channel-model sign convention, a single-client control port, and a loopback traffic path — with their diagnoses and remedies, offered as reusable lessons; and (3) an empirical characterization, over a 7.5-hour LoS campaign, that establishes UAV altitude as the primary coverage lever and quantifies device-budget, traffic-class, and repositioning effects. We deliberately restrict this study to an obstacle-free field with persistent line of sight — the cleanest setting in which to isolate the altitude/range/link-budget relationships — and treat NLoS and cluttered environments as future work (Section VIII).

II. Related Work

Al-Hourani et al. [4] derived the widely used LoS-probability model for low-altitude aerial platforms and showed that an optimal altitude balances free-space loss against shadowing; our channel model adopts their sigmoid formulation (Section III-B). Surveys by Zeng et al. [2] and Mozaffari et al. [3] catalogue design opportunities for UAV-assisted networks, and 3GPP has studied aerial channel characteristics for cellular UAV support [5]. These works are predominantly analytic or link-level. Our study complements them with protocol-stack-in-the-loop measurement over real traffic. On the tooling side, OpenAirInterface (OAI) provides an open, 3GPP-compliant RAN whose rfsimulator backend and runtime channel-model interface enable controlled, reproducible experiments [6]; Open5GS supplies the 5G core [7]; and Gazebo provides the robotics physics [8]. To our knowledge, the specific combination — physics-simulated multi-robot mobility continuously modulating a live 5G SA stack that carries measured bidirectional application traffic — has not previously been characterized for the SAR context.

III. System Architecture and Methodology

A. Testbed Overview

The testbed runs three concurrent loops on a single host. The channel loop (2 Hz) reads the poses of the UAV and robots from Gazebo, computes per-link A2G path loss, and injects it into the running gNB and UE processes. The traffic loop (one cycle per robot every ~20 s) drives ground robots along continuous trajectories and measures TCP, UDP-video, ping, and C2 metrics. The mobility loop flies the UAV through a waypoint schedule and steers the robots by differential-drive velocity commands. Two TurtleBot3 robots serve as UEs, denoted Alpha and Bravo; a PX4 x500 airframe carries the gNB. The RAN is OAI (band n78, 106 physical resource blocks, 30 kHz sub-carrier spacing) over rfsimulator, with an Open5GS core.

B. Geometry and the Air-to-Ground Channel Model

The channel is driven entirely by the instantaneous geometry, which we compute from the simulator's ground-truth poses. Let the UAV be at horizontal position (xu, yu) and altitude hu, and a robot at (xr, yr) and height hr. The horizontal distance, which is what an operator reads off a map, is

dh = √[ (xu − xr)² + (yu − yr)² ](1)

while the slant distance actually traversed by the radio wave, and the elevation angle of the UAV as seen from the robot, are

d3D = √[ dh² + (hu − hr)² ](2)
θ = arctan[ (hu − hr) / dh ]  (degrees)(3)

The distinction between (1) and (2)–(3) is central to this paper: two robots at equal dh can experience very different links if the UAV altitude, and hence θ, differs. The free-space path loss at carrier frequency f (in Hz) over the slant path is

LFSPL = 20 log10(d3D) + 20 log10(f) − 147.55(4)

Following Al-Hourani et al. [4], the probability of a line-of-sight condition as a function of elevation angle is the sigmoid

PLoS(θ) = 1 / ( 1 + a·exp[ −b(θ − a) ] )(5)

with environment constants a = 9.61 and b = 0.16. The total A2G path loss adds a LoS/NLoS excess term weighted by (5):

L = LFSPL + PLoS·ηLoS + (1 − PLoS)·ηNLoS(6)

where ηLoS = 1 dB and ηNLoS = 20 dB. Because the physical world is obstacle-free (Section IV), the geometry is always line-of-sight; the term (5)–(6) therefore governs the residual excess loss that grows as the elevation angle falls, rather than hard blockage. This is exactly why altitude is influential: raising hu increases θ, drives PLoS toward unity, and removes up to ≈19 dB of NLoS excess — an effect our measurements confirm (Section V-C).

The value injected into OAI is a per-link attenuation relative to a fixed reference. With a reference path loss Lref = L(d = 50 m, h = 30 m) and a per-link baseline Lbase (8 dB for Alpha's uplink, 13 dB for Bravo's — the 5 dB offset emulating a weaker device class), the applied attenuation is exponentially smoothed and clipped:

A = clip( Lbase + (L − Lref), 0, 45 )  dB(7)

An exponential moving average (smoothing factor 0.3) is applied to A across the 2 Hz updates to avoid discontinuous link jumps on fast pose changes.

C. Channel Injection and the Gain-Sign Convention

The bridge writes A into the live gNB and UE via OAI's channel-model control interface. A subtlety here materially affects correctness, and we record it because it is easy to get wrong and hard to detect.

Issue faced & fix What we expected: that OAI's channelmod path_loss_dB field would attenuate the signal. What actually happened: the field is applied as a linear gain — the simulator multiplies each I/Q sample by 10(path_loss_dB/20) — so a positive value amplifies the signal until sample clipping, rather than attenuating it. Injecting positive "loss" therefore produced a link that still degraded with distance (via clipping distortion, not attenuation), superficially resembling correct behaviour but inverting the physics; the tell-tale symptom was UE-reported RSRP that rose with distance. How we fixed it: the bridge keeps A internally as a positive attenuation magnitude and negates it once at the injection boundary (applying gain −A); the receiver noise floor is set to −40 dB to give attenuation a realistic dynamic range to erode SNR into; and baselines are calibrated so the link is healthy at 0 dB attenuation and reaches RLF near 35 dB. After the fix, UE-measured RSRP falls monotonically with distance (Section V-A), confirming true attenuation.

D. Honest Bidirectional Traffic via Network Namespaces

Because gNB, UEs, core, and measurement tools share one host, the UE tunnel addresses are initially local to that host, and Linux delivers locally-addressed traffic over loopback rather than the radio.

Issue faced & fix What happened: every base-to-robot packet (commands, TCP acknowledgments, any reverse-direction test) silently bypassed the emulated radio. A downlink iperf3 reported 1.4–5.2 Gbit/s while the gNB's own MAC counter showed 0.15 Mbit/s scheduled; our initial UDP video test, configured in reverse (downlink) mode, logged its full ≈100 Mbit/s offered rate at every distance, carrying no channel information at all. Fix: after attach, each UE's tunnel interface is moved into a dedicated network namespace (the modem process retains its file descriptor across the move, so the RAN is untouched). Base-to-robot traffic then measurably traverses core→gNB→radio (30 Mbit/s at the application against 52.9 Mbit/s on the gNB downlink counter at MCS 27), and the video test was reoriented as an uplink stream to match the SAR scenario, in which video flows robot-to-base. All reported traffic was collected after this fix.

E. Control-Plane Integrity: A Single-Client Control Port

Issue faced & fix What happened: OAI's telnet control interface accepts a single client per port. The bridge initially opened one connection per channel model; because both uplink models share the gNB's single port, its second connection completed the TCP handshake into the listen backlog but was never accepted, and every command written to it was discarded without error. One UE's uplink channel was therefore never modulated — its throughput held at the startup value regardless of geometry, an artifact that convincingly imitated a "weaker, budget-limited device." How it was caught: after the sign fix (Section III-C), the affected UE — carrying more attenuation than its peer — was outperforming it at long range, which is physically impossible. Fix: a single shared connection per port, plus a start-up read-back check: after writing each model's baseline the bridge queries the channel state and refuses to start unless every value reads back correctly. All measurements reported here passed this check. The general lesson across Sections III-C–III-E is to verify that control-plane writes to an emulator take effect, rather than assuming a successful socket write implies a successful command.

F. Mobility Control and Safe-Radius Governance

Robots drive by differential-drive velocity commands at 1 m/s along randomized continuous headings. The UAV flies a nine-waypoint schedule spanning altitudes {20, 40, 60} m and four ground positions, dwelling at each. To prevent robots from being stranded outside coverage as the UAV descends (which shrinks the coverage footprint), a safe-radius controller confines each robot to a model-predicted operating radius. Inverting (6)–(7), the safe radius at altitude h is the largest dh keeping the weakest UE below a safety margin Asafe = 30 dB (≈5 dB under the RLF cliff):

Rsafe(h) = max{ dh : Lbase,B + [ L(dh, h) − Lref ] ≤ Asafe }(8)

which evaluates to 101 m at 20 m altitude, 131 m at 40 m, and 163 m at 60 m. An emergency recall returns any robot that a UAV relocation nonetheless strands.

G. Measurement Suite and Metric Definitions

Each measurement cycle records, per robot: TCP goodput (2 s test, 1 s slow-start warm-up excluded); a fixed 8 Mbit/s UDP uplink stream as a video-feed proxy (delivered rate, jitter, loss); uplink-path ICMP RTT and packet delivery; and a C2 probe of five 64-byte base-to-robot pings emulating waypoint or emergency-stop delivery. UE-reported SS-RSRP and SS-SINR are sampled fresh from the modem log at each cycle. Every row is tagged with the true mid-window 3-D geometry of both robots and the UAV, from which (1)–(3) are computed. Estimated downlink RSRP is Pref − (A − Δ), with Δ the fixed uplink/downlink baseline offset, used only to cross-check the measured value. Coverage probability is the fraction of samples delivering ≥1 Mbit/s; outage probability its complement at total loss; connectivity reliability the fraction of mission time a robot remains connected. Inter-robot fairness uses the Jain index over per-robot mean goodput,

J = ( Σxi )² / ( n · Σxi² )(9)

with xi the mean goodput of robot i.

V. Results

A. Emulation Fidelity and Validation

We validate the emulated channel three ways. First, against theory: measured injected path loss tracks the analytic model (4)–(7) to 1.1–1.2 dB mean absolute error across the full 3-D geometry (Fig. 1). The residual reflects genuine variance from the noise floor and link adaptation rather than a deterministic formula echoing itself. Second, against the modem's own physics: UE-reported SS-RSRP — a quantity the bridge does not set — falls monotonically with distance and correlates with injected path loss at −0.94 (Alpha) and −0.99 (Bravo), spanning −42 dBm at closest approach to −66 dBm at the edge. Third, against itself: the fixed run at 40 m and the 40 m segment of the nomadic run, collected hours apart, produce near-identical throughput–distance curves (Fig. 2), agreeing to within ~1 Mbit/s per distance bin. Together these establish that the behaviours below arise from emulated physics, not injection artifacts.

B. Fixed versus Nomadic Operation

Fig. 3 contrasts the two regimes directly. With the UAV fixed at 40 m (Fig. 3a), Alpha's throughput holds near the ≈12 Mbit/s test ceiling out to ~85 m, then collapses over ~30 m to ~3 Mbit/s — a coverage cliff, not a gentle roll-off. When the UAV is free to change altitude (Fig. 3b), the entire cliff translates with height: its knee moves from ~65 m at 20 m altitude, to ~95 m at 40 m, to ~125 m at 60 m. The nomadic node does not so much raise throughput at a given point as relocate the boundary of the usable region.

C. Altitude as the Primary Coverage Control

Fig. 4 places each UAV altitude on its own axis, plotting both robots. The pattern is consistent: at every altitude, throughput holds near the ceiling then falls at a knee whose location advances with height, and the weaker UE (Bravo) tracks below the stronger throughout. Table II isolates the effect by holding horizontal distance fixed and varying only altitude. Close to the node altitude is nearly irrelevant; at mid-range (60–90 m) climbing 20→60 m removes 12 dB of path loss and nearly triples throughput (4.4→11.8 Mbit/s); at far range (90–120 m) the same climb raises throughput almost fivefold (2.4→11.6 Mbit/s) and lifts measured RSRP by 8 dBm. The mechanism is exactly (3)–(6): a steeper elevation angle drives PLoS up and strips out NLoS excess, as the per-altitude path-loss panels of Fig. 5 show — the model curve is markedly flatter at 60 m than at 20 m. Expressed via (8), the safe radius grows from 101 m to 163 m across the altitude range.

D. Device Budget, Coverage, and Outages

The two UEs differ only by a 5 dB baseline budget, yet experience qualitatively different networks. Alpha is distance-limited — goodput declines smoothly from ≈12 to ≈6 Mbit/s across range. Bravo is budget-limited — capped lower by its own radio and reaching the cliff sooner. The starkest expression is at the edge: over identical trajectories in the nomadic run, Bravo suffered 27 link outages to Alpha's one. A few-dB device gap thus relocates the coverage boundary and multiplies the outage rate; it does not merely scale throughput. Inter-robot fairness remains high (Jain index 0.97), confirming the gap is device budget, not unfair scheduling. Full metrics for both runs appear in Table III.

E. Traffic-Class Behaviour and Early Warning

The three traffic classes fail in distinct, ordered ways. The fixed-rate video proxy is all-or-nothing: delivered in full (≥7.9 of 8 Mbit/s) in 100% of live samples inside the envelope, then absent at the edge. TCP bends gracefully (≈12→5→1 Mbit/s) before the cliff. C2 command delivery is the most robust — median round trip 37–39 ms, 95th percentile ≤52 ms, indifferent to distance and altitude — until immediately before the edge, where the round trip inflates by an order of magnitude (to several hundred milliseconds) before any packet is lost. An operator therefore experiences edge approach as a fixed sequence — video drops, transfers crawl, command latency spikes — and the C2 latency rise is an intrinsic early-warning signal available without added instrumentation.

VI. Discussion

These results invert a common mental model of aerial base stations as "coverage that follows you." Coverage follows the node, and the node's altitude — not its ground track — is the lever to reach for first, because a robot far across the map but beneath a high UAV is, to the radio, not far at all (Table II). Two operational rules follow. First, when users struggle, climb before chasing: climbing extends coverage to all users at once and is faster than lateral flight. Second, because failure at the boundary is abrupt and the network signals it in advance (Section V-E), control should be predictive; the C2-latency and HARQ-rate leading indicators, together with the altitude lever and the hard safe-radius constraint (8), are exactly the ingredients for a learning-based nomadic-node controller, for which the labelled corpus collected here is intended as a training substrate. A corollary for repositioning: since coverage moves with the node and descent shrinks it, the safe manoeuvre is climb–translate–descend, descending last when users are closest.

VII. Challenges and Limitations

Scope: open-field line-of-sight only. The dominant limitation is the propagation environment. Every measurement was taken in an obstacle-free field where the UAV–robot link is line-of-sight throughout; the NLoS term of (6) is present but, given the geometry, never dominates. The findings therefore characterize the clean A2G regime — the setting in which the altitude/range/budget relationships are cleanest to isolate — and do not yet speak to urban canyons, forest canopy, rubble, or building shadowing, where multipath and blockage would reshape the coverage edge and could complicate the altitude lever (a higher UAV sees over some obstacles but lengthens the slant path). The quantitative edges reported here (safe radii, cliff distances) should be read as best-case, LoS figures.

Further caveats. The RAN runs over rfsimulator; path loss and delay are injected at runtime but fast fading beyond the configured profiles is absent, and the numeric edges are specific to this configuration (n78, 106 PRB, single cell, two UEs) and the calibrated baselines of Section III-C. The UAV holds altitude as a static body rather than under flight dynamics, and robot speeds (1 m/s) exceed the real TurtleBot3 specification. SS-SINR is available and reproduces the 5 dB device gap (Alpha 39 dB vs. Bravo 34), but its absolute values are compressed by the AWGN profile and closed-loop power control and are not treated as calibrated. RSRQ, F1/midhaul metrics (no CU/DU split), UAV energy, NR-U coexistence, and a simulation-to-hardware gap are out of scope for this phase. The reported model MAE is an internal consistency check, not a sim-to-hardware validation. Subject to the LoS scope, none of these affects the direction of the findings, which follow from geometry, link budget, and protocol-state mechanisms that carry over to physical LoS deployments.

VIII. Conclusion and Future Work

We presented a mobility-coupled 5G emulation testbed for UAV-mounted base stations serving ground robots and, over a 7.5-hour open-field line-of-sight campaign validated to 1.2 dB against theory and corroborated by the modem's own RSRP, established that UAV altitude is the primary coverage control: climbing recovers link margin, triples mid-range throughput, and moves the coverage cliff and safe radius outward far more effectively than horizontal pursuit. We further showed that a small device-budget gap multiplies the outage rate, that traffic classes fail in an ordered sequence that provides early warning, and that node repositioning must be coordinated to avoid stranding users; and we documented three silent-failure pitfalls of single-host RAN emulation with their remedies. The central next step is to relax the line-of-sight assumption — introducing realistic clutter and an NLoS-dominant channel to determine how much of the clean-field coverage rule survives — alongside a dedicated coverage-edge recovery-time study, a CU/DU split to bring midhaul metrics online, and eventual software-defined-radio hardware validation.
