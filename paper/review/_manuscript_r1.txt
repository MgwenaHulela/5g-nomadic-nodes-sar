
abstract
An unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) carrying a 5G base station can serve search-and-rescue robots, but it must be
managed autonomously, without knowing the terrain. We
built a terrain-blind, learning-based management layer for such a cell, which
forecasts link failure and places the UAV in three dimensions, and flew it in
closed loop on an OpenAirInterface testbed serving four robots.
Tested on held-out terrain, with a same-policy null control for comparisons
across nights, the largest gain came not from learning but from fixing a silent
failure of the managed system. Long forest outages turned
out to be uplink queuing inside the user equipment (UE) software, not coverage
loss: constant-rate video exceeded weak links, and command-and-control (C2)
traffic waited minutes behind it. A per-robot uplink cap driven only by
round-trip time and bytes sent removed these outages: in a forest patrol it raised C2 availability from
66.8% to 91.8% and cut the time with no robot connected from 17.4% to
0.9%, and it improved both placement arms flown with and without it. After censoring a second
silent failure, dead UE processes that contaminated 12.4% of observations, a linear
model forecasts C2 failure 20s ahead with modest skill (ROC-AUC 0.667) that
falls as model capacity grows, and it does not recover how terrain changes the
effect of altitude. In closed loop, no learned placement beat fixed hover or the
robot centroid; a cautious altitude learner behaved like hover. Bounding
queues and checking liveness should precede learning where to fly.

Introduction
sec:intro

After an earthquake, flood or fire, the ground robots sent to search a
disaster site need a mission-critical link for command and control (C2) and
video, and the terrestrial network they would use is often gone or
overloaded murphy2014disaster. A 5G base station (gNB) carried by an
unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) can bring a private cell to the
robots zeng2016wireless,mozaffari2019tutorial, but someone, or
something, must manage it: decide where to fly it, notice when a robot is
about to lose its link, and keep the links usable under the load the robots
put on them. On site there is no operator to tune it and no map of a terrain
that may have just been changed, so this management has to be autonomous,
proactive and blind to the terrain.

Our earlier testbed study makhubela2026satnac showed that terrain
decides whether moving such a node helps: a roaming node raised a weak robot's
C2 availability in open field and cost about 22 points in forest. A natural next
step is a learning-based management layer that predicts link failures and
places the UAV in three dimensions, without being told the terrain. This paper
builds that layer, flies it in closed loop over a real 5G stack, and asks what
it can and cannot deliver. The answer turned out to depend less on the learning
than on whether the managed system itself was measured and loaded soundly.

We test four claims, each against an explicit control (Section sec:setup):
itemize
[C1] Long forest outages under robot video are caused by
uplink queuing inside the user equipment (UE), not by coverage, and a delay-adaptive
uplink cap at each robot removes them without starving strong links.
[C2] A terrain-blind model forecasts the onset of C2 failure
20s ahead on an unseen terrain better than chance, and higher-capacity models
do worse.
[C3] The same model does not recover how terrain changes the
effect of altitude, so it cannot choose altitude.
[C4] Learned placement does not beat fixed hover or the robot
centroid, and a cautious altitude learner does no worse than hover.
itemize
C1 holds; C2 and C4 hold weakly; C3 holds as a negative result.

Contributions. The work falls under wireless and 5G networks,
mission-critical networks, proactive and autonomic management, AI-based
management, monitoring and measurement, and testbed experimentation. Beyond
the testbed and terrain comparison of makhubela2026satnac, it
contributes:
enumerate
A diagnosis and an autonomic fix. We show that the forest
outages are a silent queueing failure of the managed system. A per-robot cap
driven only by round-trip time and bytes sent, with no change to the radio
access network (RAN), raised forest C2 availability from 66.8% to 91.8% and
improved both placement arms flown with and without it (Section sec:r-cap).
A measurement-integrity detector. A second silent failure, the
UE process dying after radio-link failure, contaminated 12.4% of training
observations; we give a mechanistic rule that removes it
(Section sec:dead).
An evaluation of terrain-blind learning for proactive management,
on held-out terrain and in closed loop, with the negative results stated:
modest forecasting skill, worse with capacity, and no recovery of the
terrain-dependent altitude effect (Sections sec:r-forecast
and sec:r-flip).
A closed-loop account of learned placement, showing why a single
mission is too short to learn the forest's altitude effect and why a cautious
learner reduces to hover (Section sec:r-place).
enumerate
All results come from emulation over real 5G software; a hardware trial is the
next step.

Background and Related Work
sec:related

Aerial base stations and their testbeds. The elevation-dependent
line-of-sight model alhourani2014modeling underlies most work on where
to place a UAV base station, from 3-D placement boryaliniz2016efficient,
kalantari2016number and trajectory design wu2018common to deep
reinforcement learning qiu2020placement; surveys cover the wider
field zeng2016wireless,mozaffari2019tutorial. These works optimise
coverage or rate under an analytic or simulated channel. Testbeds now run
OpenAirInterface on UAVs with O-RAN control moore2024aerpaw or emulate
5G mobility with ray tracing iye2025owdt. Our earlier
study makhubela2026satnac coupled a real 5G stack to a terrain
simulator and found that terrain decides whether moving the node helps.

Learning-based and autonomous management. Zero-touch management aims
at fully autonomous operation under human oversight coronado2022zsm.
Machine learning for link-quality estimation cerar2021lqe, fault
management mulvey2019fault and failure prediction in 5G RANs, with deep
and transformer models islam2023dnn,hasan2024gentrap, learns from the
history of a deployed network. A UAV arriving at a disaster site has no such
history for its terrain, so we test on an unseen terrain and in closed loop.

Buffering in cellular links. Buffers that hold rather than drop
packets inflate delay gettys2012bufferbloat, and per-user cellular
buffers are a known source jiang2012tackling. Remedies range from active
queue management nichols2012codel and delay-aware rate
control winstein2013sprout to, recently, RAN-side congestion marking:
in 5G, time spent in the radio link control (RLC) buffer dominates one-way
delay wan2025l4span, and retransmission and uplink-scheduling buffers can
add delay comparable to congestion liu2025fog. Those remedies change
network queues, the RAN or the transport protocol. Our cap applies the classic additive-increase,
multiplicative-decrease (AIMD) rule chiu1989aimd to a delay signal at the robot, because constant-rate
robot video does not back off and the UE's buffer is not ours to change.


System and Method
sec:method

Platform
sec:platform
The managed system is the protocol-in-the-loop testbed
of makhubela2026satnac, extended from two to four robots
(Fig. fig:loop). An OpenAirInterface (OAI) nikaein2014oai gNB and
four OAI UEs run over the OAI radio simulator and register to an
Open5GS open5gs core (OAI commit 62fafd5860). The cell uses band n78 at
3.32GHz with 106 resource blocks at 30kHz spacing (40MHz) and a 5-ms
time-division pattern of five downlink and four uplink slots; every simulated
channel is additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN), so there is no multipath. Each UE's virtual network (TUN) interface sits in its own Linux
network namespace, so robot traffic crosses the real 5G user plane. Gazebo
moves the UAV and four ground robots; a bridge reads their poses every 0.5s
and writes a terrain-specific air-to-ground path loss into each UE's channel:
free-space loss with an elevation-dependent line-of-sight
term alhourani2014modeling, plus foliage weissberger1982,
diffraction itup526 or building loss by terrain, and correlated
shadowing gudmundson1991 (details in makhubela2026satnac). A
watchdog relaunches a UE whose process exits after radio-link failure.

Measurement Integrity: Dead-Process Detection
sec:dead
In OAI, a radio-link failure terminates the UE process. Before the watchdog
existed, the robot's C2 then stayed down for the rest of the run, and the gNB's
per-UE counters froze. Those cycles are labelled as link failures, but they
record a dead process, and a model with lagged features detects them trivially
(the first difference of the block error rate is exactly zero). We flag a cycle
as dead when, for at least three consecutive cycles, the gNB counters
dlbler, ulnbrbs and ulbitspersym are
bit-identical to the previous cycle and C2 is down. The conjunction
matters: frozen counters on a working link are a quiet cycle, and a failed
cycle with moving counters is a real degradation. The rule flags 12.4% of
all cycles and 72.2% of hills failure labels (Table tab:a1); flagged
cycles are censored from training and evaluation.


Terrain-Blind Learning Layer
sec:layer
Forecaster. The label is the onset of C2 failure: for a robot whose C2
is up at time , is it down at s? C2 is up when fewer than 50% of
its probes are lost. Features are UAV-robot geometry, gNB-side link
adaptation and error statistics, and the last commanded move, each with lags,
differences and rolling statistics within one robot's series (65 features).
Three inputs are forbidden: the simulator's injected path loss, the computed
received power (algebraically equivalent to it), and any terrain label. A guard
function checks every feature matrix before it reaches an estimator. Models are
trained leave-one-terrain-out, with thresholds and calibration chosen on an
inner time split of the training terrains.

Planner. Each 30-s decision, the planner scores candidate UAV
positions (absolute altitudes 25-55m in 5m steps, crossed with lateral
offsets of 0 or 15m) by a soft minimum over the robots' predicted risks,
 with , so the
worst-served robot dominates. It moves toward the minimiser at most 10m in
altitude per decision. The planner's risk model is the same logistic
regression with spline terms on altitude, distances and elevation angle and
Platt calibration (pooled held-out area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve, ROC-AUC, 0.659). In every fly-off the
planner uses the model trained on the other three terrains, never on the
terrain it flies over.

Altitude learner. Because the model cannot choose altitude
(Section sec:r-flip), altitude is learned during the mission. The learner
holds an incumbent altitude and rewards each altitude by the fraction of robots
with C2 up. Its estimates are time-blocked (240s blocks), so only comparisons
made close in time count. Every few decisions it probes a neighbouring altitude
(5 or 10m), and it moves when the lower confidence bound of the
neighbour-minus-incumbent difference is above zero (). The
cautious variant adds a home altitude, the hover altitude of 40m: a
move further from home must clear 1.5 standard errors and a 0.02 gain,
a move back toward home only the normal bar, and probes are rarer (every 4th
decision, every 20th once all neighbours are worse).


Delay-Adaptive Uplink Cap
sec:cap
The cap moves the bottleneck from the UE into the robot's kernel, where it can
be bounded and prioritised, and lets its rate follow the link. It needs no
change to the RAN or core. On each robot's TUN interface, a hierarchical token bucket (HTB) shaper has a
ceiling equal to the cap . C2 and ping (ICMP) traffic use a high-priority class with a
20-packet queue; video uses a low-priority class with a 10-packet queue, so
excess video is dropped rather than buffered. Each robot pings the core every
0.2s. ICMP bypasses the kernel video queue, so its round-trip time (RTT) rises only when the
UE's own buffer backs up. With  the minimum RTT over 60s and 
the median over the last second, Algorithm alg:cap runs every 0.2s.
The "in use" test keeps an idle robot's cap from rising and then overloading
the link when video resumes. The controller re-creates the shaper when a UE
reattaches with a new TUN. Its only inputs, RTT and bytes sent, are available
on real hardware.

Experimental Setup
sec:setup

Traffic and metrics. Each robot sends constant-rate UDP (User Datagram Protocol) video at
1.5Mb/s to the core and is probed for C2 by ping; a robot's C2 is up when
fewer than 50% of its probes are lost. These definitions differ from those
of makhubela2026satnac (two robots, 8Mb/s video bursts, C2 up when
any of five echoes returns), so numbers are not comparable across the two
papers. Placement runs are reported as the time-weighted share of a mission
with mean C2 up, with all four robots up, and with no robot up, and the
90th-percentile video latency. Forecasts are scored by the area under the
precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) relative to the no-skill rate (lift) and by ROC-AUC, pooled over held-out terrains and on the
worst held-out terrain.

Data. The forecasting and altitude-effect results use 54,572
robot-cycles from 20 missions of 90-200min over four terrains (open field,
urban, forest, hills), all flown before the cap existed. The cap was tested in
a static load test in forest and open field (6min of video between 2-min
no-load phases), in a 30-min forest patrol, and in the placement fly-offs.

Placement arms. Every arm flies the same pilot, seed and 90-min
mission and differs only in how x-y and altitude are chosen
(Table tab:arms): the pilot decides every 30s, changes altitude by at
most 10m per decision, and stays within a 20-60m geofence. The 40-m hover altitude and the
20-60m band are those of makhubela2026satnac: 40m is the middle of
the band and clears every obstacle in the outdoor worlds. Arm B, the
robot centroid at fixed altitude, is the baseline of our research proposal; arm
A, fixed hover, is the simplest policy; the repositioning policy
of makhubela2026satnac is a further baseline for the risk planner.

Validation protocol. A result is accepted only after it survives the
control that matches its threat: leave-one-terrain-out evaluation with
worst-fold reporting for forecasting; censoring of dead-process cycles for every
label; a same-policy null control (the same comparison applied to two nights of
one policy) for closed-loop comparisons made across nights, where a
comparison counts only if the null control's interval rules out a swing as
large as the effect; and the uplink cap
for any forest outcome. Confidence intervals are 95% block bootstraps over

not established. Per-arm placement comparisons use one 90-min run per arm and

Results
sec:results

C1: Uplink Queuing, Not Coverage, and an Autonomic Cap
sec:r-cap

The long forest outages are a queue inside the UE. With the four
robots parked under canopy (24-30dB uplink attenuation) and the UAV at
40m, C2 was clean without load. Once the four 1.5Mb/s video feeds started,
uplink ping RTT grew from about 60ms to minutes within the first seconds and
kept rising for the whole load period, peaking at 350-444s
(Fig. fig:rtt(a)); 64-91% of pings were lost and, for 64% of the
time, all eight probes (one per direction per robot) were lost at once (Table tab:static). Probes were
delayed rather than dropped, and the links drained only 0.13-0.49Mb/s of the
1.5Mb/s offered. The kernel queue on each robot's TUN interface held no backlog and dropped
nothing, so the excess was buffered beyond the kernel, inside the OAI UE
software (E1); we did not instrument which UE layer holds it. After the video stopped, C2 stayed dead for 14 to more than 18min,
consistent with each link slowly draining its backlog.

Bounding the queue removes the outages. A fixed 100kb/s cap below
every link's drain rate, applied at the same placement, removed all C2 loss and
the recovery tail, but discarded most of the video (Table tab:static).
The adaptive cap of Section sec:cap also gave zero C2 loss while settling
at a different rate on each link (medians 105-429kb/s, 32-40 cuts per robot
in 6min), delivering 0.10-0.42Mb/s of video at a median latency of
0.30-0.42s on three robots; the fourth, with the weakest link, ran at
1.08s, the time its 10-packet video queue takes to drain at about 100kb/s
(Fig. fig:rtt(b)). In open field, with 2-9dB of attenuation, every cap
rose to its 1.6Mb/s ceiling, made no cuts, and carried the full 1.55Mb/s
per robot at 82-89ms median latency (E4): in this test, where links were
strong, the cap stayed out of the way.


The cap lifts the like-for-like placement arms. In a 30-min forest patrol with
the UAV hovering at 40m, the cap raised mean C2 availability from 66.8% to
91.8% against the same seed uncapped, cut the time with no robot connected
from 17.4% to 0.9%, and the 90th-percentile video latency from 73s to
1.6s (Table tab:a5). In the forest fly-off (Fig. fig:caparms), only arms A and B flew
the same policy with and without the cap: there, the time with no robot
connected fell from 25.4 and 25.9% to 0.3 and 0.0%, and mean C2 rose from
66.6 and 58.0% to 89.1 and 88.9%. Arms C and D also changed altitude
learner between the two conditions, so their improvement cannot be attributed
to the cap alone. Each comparison is one run per condition on different
nights, with no null control for those nights.

C2: Terrain-Blind Forecasting Has Modest Skill, and Capacity Hurts
sec:r-forecast

A linear model forecasts failure onset 20s ahead on unseen terrain,
weakly. After dead-process censoring (Section sec:dead), the best
model, an L1-regularised logistic regression on 65 terrain-blind features,
reaches a pooled PR-AUC 1.79 times the no-skill rate and ROC-AUC 0.667, and no
held-out terrain falls below ROC-AUC 0.538 (Fig. fig:capacity). Skill
peaks at a 20-s horizon: a six-feature trend model scores ROC-AUC 0.650 at
20s and 0.552 at 60s (Table tab:a2). Forest labels in this data
predate the cap and are partly queue backlog (Section sec:r-cap).

More capacity is worse. On identical folds and features the ordering

Under leave-one-terrain-out validation with four terrains, we take worst-fold
skill, not pooled skill, as the figure of merit; our reading is that it rewards
models that learn a direction rather than thresholds tied to the training

Limitations and Threats to Validity
sec:limits

The radio is real 5G software, but propagation is injected: the channel has
large-scale loss and correlated shadowing but no multipath, terminal height is
fixed at 0.5m, and absolute availability is internally consistent rather than
externally calibrated. The OAI UE may buffer uplink data differently from a
commercial modem, and constant-rate video is a worst case for the queue;
whether C1 transfers is the first question for hardware. Every
between-terrain claim rests on four terrains. The forecasting and
altitude-effect data predate the cap, so their forest labels are partly queue
backlog. Placement comparisons use one 90-min run per arm with within-run
intervals, and the robots' safety guards react to link state, so the same seed
does not give identical patrols across arms. Appendix app:threats gives
the full list.

Conclusion
sec:conclusion

We built a terrain-blind, learning-based management layer for a UAV-borne 5G
cell serving four search-and-rescue robots and flew it in closed loop over real
5G software. The most effective management action was not learned: a per-robot
uplink cap driven by round-trip time removed a silent queueing failure inside
the UE and improved both placement arms flown with and without it (C1). The learning layer forecasts
link-failure onset 20s ahead with modest skill that falls as model capacity
grows (C2), does not recover how terrain changes the effect of altitude (C3),
and, in closed loop, does not beat fixed hover or the robot centroid; made
cautious, it behaves like hover (C4). For autonomous management of such
cells, the lesson is to verify that the managed system is measured and loaded
soundly, bounding its queues and checking that its processes are alive, before
learning where to fly it. The next steps are a hardware trial of the cap and
retraining the layer on capped, watchdog-protected data.
